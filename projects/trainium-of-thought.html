Trainium of Thought: co-designing a model and its training system

A fixed 30-minute training budget turns architecture, optimization, input pipeline, numerical format, and kernel launch overhead into one coupled objective.

Abstract

This project optimizes language-model validation bits per byte after exactly 30 minutes of training on Trainium2. The effective objective is not lowest loss per step or maximum tokens per second; it is the best model reachable before the wall clock expires. Starting from a 1.3951 submission, repeated controlled runs produced a 0.9669 local best and a public leaderboard peak at first place. The final recipe combines a wider, shallower model, large device batches, structured optimizer groups, streaming data, FP8 projection paths, fused attention and normalization kernels, and launch-overhead reduction.

1. The wall clock changes the optimization problem

Every design choice has two effects: it changes how much the model learns from one update, and it changes how many updates fit into 30 minutes. A feature that improves matched-step loss can still lose because it adds 20 ms to every step. Conversely, removing too much attention increases throughput but damages modeling quality more than the extra updates recover.

The primary measurement was full-budget validation bpb. Short probes screened catastrophic ideas and profilers localized cost, but promising changes had to survive the full training horizon and the exact scorer path.

2. Experimental method

Experiments were recorded in a run ledger with architecture, batch size, optimizer settings, kernel stack, seconds per step, completed updates, and final score. Kernel work used isolated microbenchmarks to understand hardware behavior, followed by a trainer-level test because faster operators can lose after integration through synchronization, staging, or host overhead.

Architecture search

Shallower, wider configurations were strong because they preserved capacity while reducing serial layer overhead. Attention-layer removal was tested carefully: one skip could be beneficial, while additional removals crossed a quality floor. U-Net-style residual skips cost memory traffic but delivered enough per-step quality to remain in the final design.

Optimization and data

Large device batches increased accelerator utilization. Muon handled major matrices while AdamW covered embeddings, output heads, and smaller parameters. Streaming data and scorer-shaped rows reduced input mismatch. Warmup and cooldown were tuned against the fixed number of steps rather than conventional epoch counts.

3. Final training stack

  • Model: approximately 1024-wide, eight-layer language model with selective attention placement and skip connections.
  • Parallel execution: four processes at LNC=2 with a 262k-token effective batch.
  • Precision: BF16 state with FP8 projection paths where numerical error remained acceptable.
  • NKI kernels: fused normalization, RoPE, cross entropy, ReLU², flash attention, embedding scatter, and selected optimizer tails.
  • Launch reduction: batched table preparation and fused residual or epilogue operations where end-to-end profiles supported them.

The FP8 v12 up-projection path stored scaled weights efficiently and folded column factors into the down projection. A later batching change reduced host launch overhead without changing losses. RoPE fusion supplied a smaller additional gain.

5. Negative results that shaped the system

Fast in isolation, slow in the trainer

A weight-resident BF16 NKI matmul beat eager operators by 6–18% in microbenchmarks but slowed the integrated training step from roughly 0.715 to 0.870 seconds. Synchronization and integration costs dominated the isolated gain.

Gates improved learning but lost the wall-clock race

Attention and smear gates led matched-step loss yet added enough runtime to finish fewer updates. Even after moving multiplications into kernels, slicing small gate inputs and launching many tiny operations consumed the advantage.

More capacity could reduce final quality

A 203M-parameter value-embedding model learned faster at matched steps but completed 11% fewer updates and ended worse. A deeper ten-layer model showed the same pattern.

Packaging was part of correctness

One built candidate was 7% slower because a flash-attention flag was omitted from the builder. Another early artifact passed local development but failed the scorer path through module alias recursion and fallback behavior. Exact artifact verification became mandatory.

6. Lessons

  1. Optimize end-of-budget quality, not a proxy in isolation.
  2. Keep per-step learning and step throughput in the same ledger.
  3. Trust trainer-level profiles over operator microbenchmarks.
  4. Treat the submission builder as production code with parity tests.
  5. Preserve negative runs; they close axes and prevent expensive repetition.
Central finding. The strongest gain came from co-design. Architecture made kernels easier to exploit, kernels changed the feasible architecture, and the fixed clock determined whether either change mattered.
